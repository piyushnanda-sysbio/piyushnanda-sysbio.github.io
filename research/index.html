Signal transduction in fluctuating environments

Incoming · Laboratory of Metabolism and Signal Transduction, EPFL

Cells perceive their world through signaling networks and set the pace of metabolism to maximize their reproduction and survival. In my PhD work, we found that the conserved Ras/cAMP/PKA pathway tunes the fraction of a yeast population that respires rather than ferments — the two strategies for producing ATP from glucose. The pathway distinguishes among carbon sources and their concentrations, and governs how yeast adapts to changing nutrient levels.

I became interested in how biophysical principles govern the way these signaling circuits evolve to adjust dynamically to changing environments. Most signaling networks are built from the same parts — chains of kinase and phosphatase cycles, wired together by positive and negative feedback. My lab will ask how that wiring determines what a cell can sense when its inputs fluctuate, and how the wiring changes as networks evolve. We are developing a framework that predicts the response of a cascade with arbitrary feedback directly from its wiring, and testing it with single-cell measurements in yeast.

A four-level signaling cascade reading a fluctuating input, X0(t). Each level is a protein switched on by a kinase and off by a phosphatase, and each active protein switches on the next. Two feedback loops act back up the chain, one positive (green) and one negative (red), and the output activates one downstream pathway while repressing another. Below: 20 residues of adenylate cyclase (Cyr1), the enzyme Ras activates to make cAMP, compared across twelve yeast species. A few positions are identical or nearly so in every species, suggesting strong constraint; others differ between species. Whether any of these differences change how the pathway behaves is an open question.

Feedback control of single-cell energy metabolism

Incoming · ELISIR Scholar, EPFL School of Life Sciences

A fast-growing cell turns over its entire ATP pool in well under a second. That makes energy metabolism acutely exposed to a glucose supply that, outside the lab, almost never holds still — a tumor cell moving through heterogeneous tissue, or yeast in a bioreactor, has to keep ATP stable while its environment swings. We understand the fermentation–respiration decision fairly well for populations at steady state. We understand much less about how an individual cell manages the transition.

The group I am starting at EPFL will ask what a cell must sense in order to set glycolytic flux that both maximizes growth and defends ATP levels. The approach is to measure processes rather than components: calibrated biosensors imaged in single cells from seconds to hours, combined with yeast genetics.

The kind of measurement this is built on. Left: cells held under a microfluidic flow that switches glucose for galactose/ethanol. Right: ATP:ADP in the same field, read out with the PercevalHR sensor. Every cell loses ATP within seconds of the shift; over hours, Recoverers (R) restore it while Arrestors (A) next to them do not.

Three directions. First, the allosteric controls on phosphofructokinase — conserved across the tree of life, yet removable with surprisingly little cost at steady state. I suspect they exist to buffer ATP against fluctuation rather than to set mean flux. Using calibrated ATP sensors, my group will test whether these allosteric controls buffer ATP levels against stochastic glucose pulses in a microfluidic setup. Imaging at high frequency and single-cell resolution will give new insight into the dynamics of energy metabolism. Second, the reverse direction: whether energy state feeds back onto the signaling networks that are supposed to be reading the environment. Third, how mutations in those networks reshape the glucose-in, ATP-out relationship, and what that does to fitness.

Metabolic bistability in yeast

PhD work · Murray Lab, Harvard · 2020–present

A clonal population of budding yeast is not metabolically uniform. Individual cells settle into distinct, heritable states of energy metabolism and stay there for many generations. I combined single-cell metabolic imaging with physiological perturbations to show that this split is generated by a bistable switch in mitochondrial activity, driven by antagonism between cytoplasmic and mitochondrial translation.

The two states are already visible before the shift. Every cell has mitochondria (matrix-targeted mNeptune, top left), but only some carry high levels of a mitochondrial sfGFP reporter (bottom left); Recoverers are outlined in cyan, Arrestors in magenta. Right: per-cell sfGFP is bimodal and separates the two groups.

Because the two states differ in how they fare when conditions change, the switch gives the population a bet-hedging strategy: it pays a cost in average growth rate in exchange for having some cells already in the right state when the environment turns over. Alongside the experiments, I developed theory and computational pipelines for tracking single cells and their metabolic state over long timescales.

From single cells to multicellular clusters

Murray Lab, Harvard · 2020–2024

Snowflake-like yeast clusters form when daughter cells fail to separate from their mothers. What sets the size and shape of the resulting clusters? I built a dynamic network model that takes cell-level properties — budding geometry, bond strength, division timing — and predicts the full distribution of cluster sizes and compositions, then tested it against high-throughput microscopy measurements of absolute cluster size.

Cell-level rules set cluster size. ace2Δ cells stay attached after division; inducing BUD4 with β-estradiol changes where they bud. Uninduced (left) versus 10 nM β-estradiol (right). Scale bars, 5 µm.

Genome-scale metabolic modeling

Metabolic Engineering Lab, IIT Kharagpur · 2018–2020

Metabolic networks are large and underdetermined, which makes it hard to say which reactions actually change between two conditions. I designed Genome Scale–Differential Flux Analysis (GS-DFA), a statistical framework that integrates genome-scale metabolic networks with transcriptomic data to identify reactions whose flux is significantly altered, and applied it to lung cells infected with SARS-CoV-2.

Earlier, I reconstructed and analyzed a genome-scale metabolic model of Lachancea kluyveri, a weak Crabtree-positive yeast, to understand how its fermentation–respiration tradeoff differs from that of S. cerevisiae.
